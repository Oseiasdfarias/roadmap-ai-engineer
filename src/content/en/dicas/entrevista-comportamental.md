## What it is

The behavioral interview asks how you acted in real situations: "tell me about a time you disagreed with someone", "tell me about a mistake you made". The answer has its own format, STAR: Situation (the context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you did) and Result (what happened and what you learned).

## Why it matters

People hiring juniors usually know you have little experience, so they look for signs of how you work: how you communicate, learn, take criticism and own mistakes. A vague answer ("I am dedicated") shows nothing. A concrete story does, and the STAR format keeps you from getting lost in the details.

You do not need professional experience. Internships, college, personal projects and group work all count as material. The MIT CAPD guide in the references has more examples of behavioral questions and how to structure the answers.

## Step by step

1. **List ten real situations**: a hard bug, a tight deadline, a disagreement, a mistake of yours, something you learned on your own, a group project.
2. **Pick five and write each one in STAR**, in four to six lines, without padding. The focus is the Action: what you did, in the first person.
3. **Include a concrete result**, with a number when there is one, and a lesson.
4. **Map the stories to common questions**: mistake, conflict, deadline, learning fast, leadership, feedback. One story can serve more than one question.
5. **Rehearse out loud**, with a timer, one to two minutes per answer.
6. **Prepare "Tell me about yourself"**: who you are, what you have built and why this role, in about one minute.
7. **Prepare two questions** for the team, about day-to-day work and how new people are supported.

## Example

Question: "Tell me about a mistake you made."

Answer, before:

> I have made mistakes a few times, but I always learned from them.

After (illustrative example):

> **Situation:** in my manual search project, I indexed all the files without checking the metadata. **Task:** I was the only person on the project and had to deliver the demo on Friday. **Action:** when testing, I saw answers citing the wrong manual. I stopped, wrote a test that checked the manufacturer and model of each passage, fixed the import and reindexed. **Result:** the citations matched the manual in all 25 test cases. I learned to validate data before indexing, and today that test runs on every change.

The answer shows a real mistake, an action of yours and a lesson applied.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering in general terms**, without a specific story.
- **Saying "we" all the time.** The interviewer needs to know what you did.
- **Giving a fake flaw**, such as "I am a perfectionist". Pick a real, small mistake and show what changed.
- **Blaming other people** for what went wrong.
- **Giving answers that are too long.** Go past two minutes and attention drops.
- **Making up a story.** Deep-dive questions reveal when something did not happen.

## Checklist

- Five stories written in STAR, with a result.
- Each story linked to two or more common questions.
- One story about a mistake, one about conflict and one about learning.
- Answers rehearsed out loud, one to two minutes each.
- "Tell me about yourself" ready, in about one minute.
- Two questions for the team prepared.
